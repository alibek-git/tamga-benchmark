# Data sources

Where every pair in the corpus comes from, under which licence, and what was deliberately
left out. Licence terms are as understood at the dates given; `[VERIFY]` against current
published terms before redistributing anything derived from them.

## 1. Watchlists — the right-hand side of every match

The lists a real screening system matches against. Only the first is used in this corpus;
the rest are listed because they share the same matching problem and are the obvious
extensions.

| List | Publisher | Access | Used here |
|---|---|---|---|
| SDN + Consolidated | US Treasury OFAC | Free, machine-readable | **Yes** — snapshot of 2026-07-24 |
| Entity List, Denied Persons, Unverified | US BIS | Free | No. Export control rather than financial sanctions; same matching problem |
| Consolidated Financial Sanctions List | EU | Free, XML | No |
| Consolidated List | UK OFSI | Free | No |
| Consolidated List | UN Security Council | Free | No |
| National lists | KZ and other jurisdictions | Varies | No. `[VERIFY]` availability and format |

Official lists are free to download but costly to normalise, deduplicate and keep fresh.
[OpenSanctions](https://www.opensanctions.org/) aggregates many of them into a single
normalised model (FollowTheMoney) and publishes the open-source matching libraries
`nomenklatura` and `rigour` — which are the serious specialist baselines in this benchmark.
Their bulk data is offered under a non-commercial licence with commercial terms available;
`[VERIFY]` before depending on it. **This corpus does not redistribute OpenSanctions bulk
data**; it uses their matcher as a baseline and the raw OFAC list as a source.

## 2. Positive pairs — where the labels come from

Pairs known to refer to the same entity across scripts. Three sources, all public, none
requiring private data.

**a) OFAC SDN alias sets.** Sanctions entries carry alternate spellings, and for
Cyrillic-origin entries those are frequently exactly the romanisation variants of interest —
curated by government analysts. Gold-standard positives, because they record how one
entity's name is actually written across systems. 1,250 pairs.

**b) Wikidata.** Every item carries labels in many languages, aligned by construction — the
`ru`, `kk`, `uk` and `en` labels of one item denote one entity by definition. The single
richest source of cross-script positives, and CC0. Queried via SPARQL, filtered to humans
and organisations with labels in at least two relevant scripts. 496 pairs.

**c) Published transliteration standards.** The rule tables themselves — ISO 9, GOST 7.79,
BGN/PCGN, ICAO 9303, KMU 55, Uzbek Latin 1995 — generate *synthetic* positives with a known
ground-truth transformation. Essential for coverage of rare letters, and **capped at 30% of
positives**: rule-generated pairs only test transformations already modelled, so a corpus
dominated by them measures its own assumptions back at itself. 745 pairs.

Uzbek Cyrillic barely exists in Wikidata (46 `uz-cyrl` labels), so Uzbek Cyrillic pairs are
generated by inverting the 1995 Latin mapping and are marked synthetic.

## 3. Hard negatives — the part that decides whether the benchmark is honest

Easy negatives — two unrelated, dissimilar names — make every matcher look excellent. The
value is almost entirely in negatives that are *string-similar but different entities*.
**1,911 pairs, 73% of all negatives**, in five constructions:

| Construction | n | What it tests |
|---|---|---|
| `negative:similar-string` | 1,365 | High string similarity after romanisation |
| `negative:same-surname` | 650 | Same surname, different given name |
| `negative:gender-pair` | 380 | Gendered forms of one surname, unrelated people |
| `negative:romanisation-collision` | 341 | Two different Cyrillic surnames collapsing to one Latin form (`Әбиев` and `Абиев` both give `abiev`) — the sharpest negative available |
| `negative:patronymic-collision` | 166 | Different entities sharing surname *and* patronymic |

Two construction rules that shape every number in the results:

- **Entity distinctness is proven, not assumed.** Negatives require both dates of birth
  present and different. Distinct identifiers alone are insufficient, because Wikidata
  contains duplicate items for one person and those share a date of birth.
- **Unresolvable negatives are excluded** — 127 pairs whose two names are identical after
  normalisation. They are correctly labelled, but no name matcher can resolve them from
  names alone, so they would add a fixed error floor that says nothing about cross-script
  matching. The count is in the manifest.

Three slices fell short of target and are reported rather than silently truncated:
`similar-string` 375/700, `romanisation-collision` 344/380, `patronymic-collision` 178/200.
These are data limits on entities with differing dates of birth.

## 4. What is not in the corpus, and never will be

- **Customer or screening-payload data.** None. Not from a pilot, not from anywhere.
- **Scraped private-individual data.** The corpus uses public figures — watchlist entries
  and Wikidata notables — and synthetic constructions. It must not become a compiled
  dataset of ordinary people's names and identifiers. That is a privacy harm and, for EU
  data subjects, a legal one. See [`legal-and-ethics.md`](legal-and-ethics.md) §4.
- **Anything with a licence prohibiting redistribution.** A single non-redistributable
  input contaminates the whole release.
- **Evaluations of commercial screening products.** See
  [`legal-and-ethics.md`](legal-and-ethics.md) §3.

## 5. Reproducibility

Source snapshots are committed to `benchmark/sources/` rather than fetched at build time,
so the corpus rebuilds **offline and byte-identically**, and CI asserts that on every
change. Fetching live would make the corpus non-reproducible, which
[`../CLAUDE.md`](../CLAUDE.md) §4 disallows: a figure must stay checkable against the exact
data it was measured on, even after the upstream list has moved on.

Sanctions data decays weekly. The OFAC snapshot is dated 2026-07-24 and the Wikidata
snapshots 2026-07-26 and 2026-08-10. Treat every figure as of those dates.

`benchmark/harness/build/fetch_ofac.py` and `fetch_wikidata.py` are the scripts that
produced the snapshots; run them to take a fresh one, which produces a **new corpus
version**, not a mutation of `v1.0`.
